Why the terms keep changing

For decades, “ESL” was the word for almost everything: the students, the classes, the office. That is no longer true. Terms for language learners change with laws, funding, research, and how students describe themselves. Each sector, from K-12 schools to universities to federal agencies, has its own preferred word.

An NCTE glossary written by five teacher educators traces part of this history. Before 2001, school documents mostly used “bilingual” or “ESL,” terms that acknowledge a first language. Federal policy then moved toward “English language learner,” and later toward “English learner” (Alvarez et al., 2017). The authors note that shifting terms can confuse instructors and can sound like jargon. They also quote a student who asked when she would stop being an English language learner and become a user of English.

The overall direction in college writing is from deficit terms, which name what a student lacks, toward asset terms, which name the languages and skills a student brings. WIDA, for example, describes “multilingual learners” as an assets-based term that emphasizes what a student knows.

For instructors, the question is practical: which word to use, with whom, and when to use none.

Seven practices

  1. Describe the skill, not the student

    In feedback and conversation, name the task: “articles and verb tense,” “reading long sentences.” You rarely need a label for the person to help them.

  2. Use the term the student uses

    If a student calls themselves bilingual, an international student, or a Spanish speaker, use their word. If they do not mention language at all, you do not need to either.

  3. Match the term to the setting

    Use a program’s or a law’s official name when you refer to that program or law. For a course name, such as an ESL course, use the course name. For general writing about students, “multilingual learners” is current and broad.

  4. Avoid deficit terms

    “Limited English proficient,” “non-native,” and “ESLs” as a noun for people describe students by a gap. Choose terms that describe a situation or a skill.

  5. Do not make the native speaker the standard

    Academic English is a learned way of writing for every student, including students who grew up speaking English. Frame expectations around audience, purpose, and genre, not around sounding like a native speaker. Separate language development from thinking: a strong argument can arrive in sentences that still show a second language. See Knowing your students’ language backgrounds.

  6. Welcome other languages in the process

    Students can brainstorm, take notes, or plan in any language they think well in, then write the final work in English. This is often called translanguaging. It is different from using a translation tool to produce the English text, which is a separate question for each assignment. See Setting expectations for translation, grammar tools, and AI.

  7. Expect the terms to change again

    No term is permanent. What matters more than the word is that it is accurate, respectful, and used only when it is needed.

How this toolkit uses the terms

Student pages use no label at all. They describe skills and tasks, so any student can use them without identifying as a language learner. Instructor pages use “multilingual learners” for students and “second language writing” when following the CCCC statement.

About these practices

These practices are practitioner advice from faculty development work at OCTC, informed by the sources below. The terms and definitions come from the sources; the advice on when to use them does not.

What changed

  • The labels are gone. Students choose the help they need without first placing themselves in a group.
  • “Limited English proficiency,” a deficit term, is gone.
  • Grammar is one skill among several. The offer covers reading and writing too.

A welcoming syllabus line

This course welcomes writers who use more than one language. If you do, you bring real strengths to our class. For feedback on any part of your writing, visit the Teaching and Learning Center, or use The Language of College for short lessons on each skill.

When a student brings up their language

Thank you for telling me. Which parts of reading or writing in this course would be most useful to work on? I can point you to resources for those.
