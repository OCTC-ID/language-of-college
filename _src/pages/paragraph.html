What a college paragraph does

In college writing, a paragraph develops one main idea. It tells the reader the idea, supports it, and explains why the support matters. When a new idea starts, a new paragraph starts.

Readers in U.S. colleges expect the main idea near the beginning of the paragraph. They read the first sentence to learn what the paragraph is about, and they expect the rest to stay on that idea.

Key terms
Topic sentence:
the sentence that states the main idea of a paragraph. It is usually the first sentence.
Support:
reasons, examples, facts, or ideas from a source that show the main idea is true.
Transition:
a word or phrase that connects one idea to the next, such as for example or as a result.

Four parts of a strong paragraph

  1. Point. The topic sentence. State the main idea in one sentence.
  2. Support. Give a reason, an example, a fact, or an idea from a source.
  3. Explain. Say how the support proves your point. This is the part writers most often skip.
  4. Link. End by connecting back to the main idea, or forward to the next paragraph.

A paragraph can repeat steps 2 and 3. A strong paragraph often has two pieces of support, each with its own explanation.

How long?

Most college paragraphs are four to eight sentences. A one-sentence paragraph usually needs more support. A paragraph that fills a whole page usually has more than one idea.

Language focus: transitions

Language focus

Words that connect ideas

A transition tells the reader how the next sentence connects to the one before it. Choose the transition that matches the connection.

Transitions by purpose
To showTransitionsExample
an examplefor example, for instanceFor example, my coworker wanted to study welding.
more of the samealso, in addition, anotherIn addition, evening classes help parents.
a resultas a result, so, thereforeAs a result, she lost a year of progress.
a contrasthowever, but, on the other handHowever, evening classes cost more to staff.
what it meansthis means that, in other wordsThis means that many students cannot attend.
Watch for this

Use a transition when it helps, not in every sentence. A paragraph where every sentence starts with a transition is hard to read. A comma usually follows a transition at the start of a sentence: As a result, she waited.

Practice

Read this paragraph. Then answer the questions.

(1) Online tutoring helps students who cannot come to campus. (2) Some students live an hour away. (3) The library has new computers. (4) A video session lets these students get help from home. (5) This saves them two hours of driving.

  1. Which sentence is the topic sentence?
  2. Which sentence does not belong? Why?
  3. Which sentence explains why the support matters?
  4. Write a last sentence that links back to the main idea.
Show suggested answers
  1. Sentence 1. It states the main idea, and the other sentences support it.
  2. Sentence 3. New computers in the library are not about students who cannot come to campus.
  3. Sentence 5. It shows what the support means: time saved.
  4. One possible answer: “For these students, online tutoring makes help possible.” It returns to the main idea. Other answers are possible.

Next step

Check one paragraph in your draft

  1. Underline the topic sentence. Does it state one main idea?
  2. Does every sentence support that idea?
  3. After each piece of support, did you explain what it shows?
  4. Does the last sentence link back or forward?
