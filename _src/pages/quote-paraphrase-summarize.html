Three ways to use a source

Many college assignments ask you to use sources: articles, books, websites, or interviews. There are three ways to bring a source into your writing. In all three, you must say where the idea came from. This is called citing the source.

Quote, paraphrase, and summary
WayWhat you writeLengthUse it when
QuoteThe author’s exact words, inside quotation marksUsually short: a phrase or one sentenceThe exact words matter, or they are hard to say better
ParaphraseOne idea from the source, in your own words and your own sentence structureAbout the same length as the originalYou need the details of one idea
SummaryThe main idea of a longer passage, in your own wordsMuch shorter than the originalYou need the big idea, not the details

In most college writing, paraphrase and summary are more common than quotation. Instructors want to see that you understand the source well enough to explain it.

Key terms
Source:
a text, person, or recording that you take information from.
Cite:
to say where an idea or words came from, in the style your instructor asks for, such as MLA or APA.
Plagiarism:
using someone else’s words or ideas without citing them.

How to paraphrase without copying

Changing a few words in the author’s sentence is not a paraphrase. If the sentence structure and most of the words stay the same, it is still too close to the original. Many instructors count this as plagiarism, even with a citation.

Too close, and a real paraphrase
OriginalToo closeReal paraphrase
Students who work more than twenty hours a week are less likely to finish their degree.Students who have jobs more than twenty hours a week are less likely to complete their degree.Working over twenty hours a week makes it harder for students to graduate.

Four steps

  1. Read the passage until you understand it. Look up any words you need.
  2. Close the source, or cover it.
  3. Write the idea from memory, as if you were explaining it to a classmate.
  4. Compare. If your version uses the same structure or many of the same words, try again. Keep special terms that have no other word, such as GPA.
Paraphrasing tools

Some websites and AI tools offer to paraphrase for you. They write the new sentence, so the tool is leading, not you. Use one only if your instructor allows it. See Translation, grammar tools, and AI: check the rules first.

Language focus: reporting verbs

Language focus

Verbs that introduce a source

When you use a source, start with a signal phrase: the author’s name or the author, plus a reporting verb. The verb tells the reader what the author does with the idea.

Reporting verbs
The authorReporting verbsExample
gives informationexplains, describes, reportsThe author explains that schedules often change.
takes a positionargues, claims, believesThe author argues that time is the real problem.
offers an idea carefullysuggests, notesThe author suggests that employers could help.
reports researchfound, showsThe study found that students who work long hours leave more often.
Watch for this

After a reporting verb, use that + a complete sentence: The author argues that time is the problem. Do not write “The author argues about that time is the problem.”

Use the present tense for what an author says (argues, explains). See Verb tense for when a class uses the past tense instead.

Practice

Use this original sentence: “Community colleges serve many adult students who return to school after years in the workforce.”

  1. Is this a quote, a paraphrase, or a summary? The author notes that many adults go back to school at community colleges after working for years.
  2. Is this a real paraphrase or too close? Community colleges help many adult students who go back to school after years in the workforce.
  3. Choose the best reporting verb for an author who takes a position: The author (describes / argues) that colleges should offer evening classes.
  4. Correct this sentence: “The author explains about that adults return to school.”
Show suggested answers
  1. A paraphrase. It gives the same idea in new words and a new structure, at about the same length. It has no quotation marks.
  2. Too close. It keeps the structure and most of the words. Only two words changed.
  3. argues Should shows a position. Describes is for information.
  4. The author explains that adults return to school. After a reporting verb, use that + a complete sentence, with no about.

Next step

Before you use a source in your writing

  1. Decide: do I need the exact words, one idea, or the main idea?
  2. If I paraphrase, did I close the source and write from memory?
  3. Did I start with a signal phrase and a reporting verb?
  4. Did I cite the source in the style my instructor asks for?
