What a complete sentence needs

A complete sentence has three parts: a subject, a verb, and a complete idea. It can stand alone and make sense.

  • I work full time. Subject (I), verb (work), complete idea.

Two kinds of problems happen at the edges of sentences:

  • A fragment is a piece of a sentence written as if it were complete. Because I work full time.
  • A run-on is two complete sentences joined with no correct connection. I work full time I take two classes. When only a comma joins them, it is called a comma splice: I work full time, I take two classes.

Both problems make readers stop and read again, because they cannot tell where one idea ends and the next begins.

Key terms
Independent clause:
a subject and verb with a complete idea. It can be a sentence by itself.
Dependent clause:
a subject and verb that start with a word such as because, when, or although. It cannot stand alone.
Fragment:
an incomplete sentence.
Run-on and comma splice:
two sentences joined incorrectly.

Finding and fixing fragments

To test a sentence, read it alone, out of the paragraph. Could you say it to someone as a complete statement? If it leaves them waiting for more, it is a fragment.

Three common fragments

  1. A dependent clause alone. It starts with a word such as because, although, when, if, or since. Because I work full time.
  2. An example alone. It starts with for example, such as, or like, but it has no verb. For example, my biology class.
  3. A missing subject or verb. Is important to plan ahead. (no subject) The students in my group. (no verb)

Two ways to fix a fragment

Fixing fragments
FragmentFix 1: join it to the sentence next to itFix 2: add what is missing
I study at night. Because I work full time.I study at night because I work full time.I work full time.
Some classes are hard. For example, my biology class.Some classes are hard, such as my biology class.For example, my biology class is hard.
Is important to plan ahead.Not neededIt is important to plan ahead.

Fixing run-ons and comma splices

When two complete sentences meet, connect them in one of four ways.

Four ways to connect two sentences
WayExample
1. A periodI work full time. I take two classes.
2. A comma + and, but, so, or, yetI work full time, but I take two classes.
3. A semicolon (;)I work full time; I take two classes.
4. A word such as because, although, whileAlthough I work full time, I take two classes.

Choose the way that shows the relationship. But and although show contrast. So shows a result. A period is always correct and is often the clearest choice.

Two common problems

Watch for these

1. However, therefore, and for example between two sentences

These words connect ideas, but they cannot join two sentences with only a comma. Use a period or a semicolon before them.

Connecting words between sentences
Not thisWrite this
The class is online, however, it has live meetings.The class is online. However, it has live meetings.
I missed the deadline, therefore, I lost points.I missed the deadline; therefore, I lost points.

2. A comma between two long sentences

When a sentence feels long, a writer may add a comma to give the reader a pause. A comma cannot hold two complete sentences together by itself. Read each half alone. If both halves are complete, use one of the four ways.

  • Comma splice: “My manager changed my schedule, I had to drop my morning class.”
  • Fixed: My manager changed my schedule, so I had to drop my morning class.

Patterns you will use often

The punctuation in each pattern is already correct. Fill in each blank with a complete idea.

With because

When because comes first, put a comma after the first part: Because I work at night, I study in the morning. When it comes in the middle, you usually do not need a comma: I study in the morning because I work at night.

Practice

Is each one a complete sentence, a fragment, or a comma splice?

  1. Although the reading was long.
  2. The reading was long, it had many new words.
  3. The reading was long, but it was interesting.
  4. For example, the second chapter.
  5. Studying with a partner helps me remember new words.

Then fix this paragraph: “I chose nursing. Because I like helping people. My first class was difficult, I studied every night. However, I passed.”

Show suggested answers
  1. Fragment. It starts with although, so it needs a second part: Although the reading was long, I finished it.
  2. Comma splice. Both halves are complete. Use a period, a semicolon, or a comma + and.
  3. Complete sentence. A comma + but joins the two sentences correctly.
  4. Fragment. It has no verb. Add one: For example, the second chapter was very long.
  5. Complete sentence. The subject is studying with a partner, and the verb is helps.

One possible answer: I chose nursing because I like helping people. My first class was difficult, so I studied every night. However, I passed. The because fragment joins the sentence before it. The comma splice gets so, which shows a result. The last sentence was already correct: However starts a new sentence. Other answers are possible.

Next step

Try this with your own writing

  1. Read your draft from the last sentence to the first. Out of order, each sentence must stand on its own.
  2. For each sentence, ask: does it have a subject, a verb, and a complete idea?
  3. Look at every comma. If the words on both sides are complete sentences, use one of the four ways.
  4. Look for however and therefore. Is there a period or a semicolon before them?
