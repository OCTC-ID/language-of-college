What a prompt tells you

A writing prompt is the set of instructions for a writing assignment. Your grade depends on doing what the prompt asks, so read it slowly, at least two times, before you start to write.

Good writers at every level sometimes answer a different question than the one the prompt asked. A careful reading prevents that. As you read, look for the answers to five questions.

  1. What do I do? Find the task verb, such as explain, compare, or argue.
  2. What do I write about? Find the topic and any limits on the topic.
  3. Who is my reader? Some prompts name a reader. If the prompt does not, write for a reader who has not taken your class.
  4. What are the requirements? Find the length, the number of sources, and the format.
  5. How do I finish? Find the due date, the place to submit your work, and how it will be graded.

If the prompt does not answer one of these questions, ask your instructor. That is a normal and useful question.

Key terms
Writing prompt:
the instructions for a writing assignment.
Task verb:
the verb that names the kind of thinking and writing you must do.
Rubric:
a chart that shows how your work will be graded.

Language focus: required, allowed, or not allowed

Language focus

Commands

Prompts often start a sentence with a verb: Write an essay. Support your argument. Submit your essay. These sentences are commands. They have no subject because the subject is always you. In a prompt, a command is not rude. It is a requirement.

Small words that change the meaning

Words such as must, should, and may tell you how much choice you have.

Watch for this

In a prompt, may not means “not allowed.” It does not mean “maybe not.”

Words that set limits

  • at least two sources: two or more.
  • no more than 1,000 words: 1,000 words or fewer.
  • 750–1,000 words: any number from 750 to 1,000.
  • one specific change: exactly one, described clearly.

Sentence starters: asking about a prompt

Instructors expect questions about prompts. Ask early, and say what you already understand. Fill in each blank with your own words.

  • To check your understandingI want to check my understanding. The essay should . Is that correct?
  • To ask about the purposeWhat skill should I practice in this assignment?
  • To ask what a word meansIn the prompt, what does “” mean?
  • To ask about a limitDoes the word count include ?
  • To ask about your topicMay I write about for this assignment?
  • To ask what is allowedAre we allowed to use for this assignment?

Practice

Read this prompt for a discussion post. Then answer the questions.

Read the assigned article about working from home. In one paragraph of 150–200 words, summarize the author’s main argument. Then, in a second paragraph, explain whether you agree with the author. Use one example from your own experience. You may write in first person (I, my). Do not use outside sources.

  1. What are the two task verbs?
  2. How many paragraphs must you write?
  3. Is it acceptable to write “I agree because my job changed last year”?
  4. You find a helpful website about working from home. Can you quote it in your post?
  5. What does the prompt not tell you?
Show suggested answers
  1. Summarize and explain. First you report the author’s main argument in fewer words. Then you give your own position and your reasons.
  2. Two. The prompt says “one paragraph” and then “a second paragraph.”
  3. Yes. “You may write in first person” means it is allowed. The prompt also asks for an example from your own experience.
  4. No. “Do not use outside sources” is a command. The only source you can use is the assigned article.
  5. The length of the second paragraph, the due date, and how the post is graded. These are good questions to ask. You could write: “Does the 150–200 word limit apply to the second paragraph too?”
