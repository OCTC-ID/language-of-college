Understand the assignment

Task Verbs: Analyze, Compare, Evaluate

Use this page when a prompt uses a verb such as analyze or evaluate, and you are not sure what it asks you to do.

About 12 minutes. Includes practice with answers.

Why the verb matters

A task verb is the word in a prompt that names the kind of thinking you must do. Two students can write about the same topic and receive very different grades, because one did what the verb asked and the other did not.

Look at one topic with three different verbs:

  • Describe the effects of social media on sleep. Report what the effects are.
  • Explain why social media affects sleep. Give the causes.
  • Evaluate one way to reduce the effects of social media on sleep. Judge how well it works, and give reasons.

These are three different assignments. A strong answer to the first one would be a weak answer to the third.

Check your course first

Instructors do not all use these verbs in exactly the same way. If your prompt or your instructor defines a verb, use that definition. If you are not sure, ask.

Key terms
Task verb:
the verb that names the kind of thinking and writing you must do.
Evidence:
facts, examples, or ideas from sources that support a point.
Criteria:
the standards you use to judge something. One standard is a criterion.

Three groups of verbs

Most task verbs belong to one of three groups. Each group asks for more of your own thinking than the one before it.

Group 1: Report

Say what something is or what a source says. Stay close to the facts. The prompt is not asking for your opinion.

Verbs that ask you to report
VerbWhat to do
defineGive the exact meaning of a word or idea.
describeSay what something is like. Give details.
identifyName the thing the prompt asks about. A short answer is often enough.
summarizeGive the main ideas of a text in fewer words, in your own words.

Group 2: Show how or why

Show how ideas connect. Your thinking is visible in the connections you make.

Verbs that ask you to show how or why
VerbWhat to do
explainMake something clear by giving reasons or steps. Answer “how?” or “why?”
analyzeBreak something into parts. Show how the parts work together.
compareShow how two things are alike. Many instructors also want the differences.
contrastShow how two things are different.
applyUse an idea from the course in a new situation.

Group 3: Take a position

Give your own judgment or view, and support it. Your opinion is required, and so are your reasons.

Verbs that ask you to take a position
VerbWhat to do
argueState a position. Support it with reasons and evidence.
evaluateJudge how good, useful, or true something is. Name your criteria and give reasons.
respondGive your own reaction to a text, with reasons.
reflectThink about your own experience or learning. Say what it means to you and what you will do next.
A verb to ask about

Discuss is the least exact task verb. It usually means: look at the topic from more than one side, and then give your view. If a prompt says only “discuss,” ask your instructor what the answer should include.

Language focus: words for comparing and judging

Language focus

Signal words for each task

Words that show the kind of thinking
TaskUseful wordsExample
Show a likenessboth, like, similarly, in the same wayBoth courses require a final project.
Show a differencehowever, while, unlike, in contrastOnline classes are flexible, while in-person classes have a fixed schedule.
Show a causebecause, so, as a result, leads toStudents sleep less, so they remember less.
Give a judgmenteffective, convincing, limited, a strength, a weaknessOne weakness of the plan is its cost.

Comparing with -er and more

To compare two things, change the adjective and add than.

  • Short adjectives add -er: cheaper than, easier than, longer than.
  • Longer adjectives use more: more flexible than, more expensive than, more effective than.
  • Two common adjectives are irregular: good, better and bad, worse.
Watch for this

Use -er or more, never both. Write easier than. Do not write “more easier than.” Also check the spelling of than. Then is a different word, about time.

Sentence starters

Fill in each blank with your own words.

  • To summarizeThe author explains that .The main idea of the article is .
  • To analyzeOne important part of is . depends on because .
  • To compare and contrastBoth and .Unlike , .
  • To evaluate is effective because .One weakness of is .
  • To argue should because .

Practice

  1. The prompt says: “Summarize the article in 100 words.” Should you say whether you agree with the author?
  2. The prompt says: “Compare online classes and in-person classes.” A student writes three good paragraphs about online classes. What is missing?
  3. Which sentence evaluates?
    • A. The article has five paragraphs.
    • B. The article is about sleep.
    • C. The article’s evidence is weak because it comes from one person’s experience.
  4. Find the two task verbs: “Describe one problem in your community and argue for a solution.” What does each verb ask you to do?
  5. Correct this sentence: “Online classes are more easier then in-person classes.”
Show suggested answers
  1. No. Summarize is a Group 1 verb. Report the author’s main ideas. Your opinion is not part of the task.
  2. The in-person classes, and the comparison. Compare needs two things and the connections between them. Writing about one thing is describing, not comparing.
  3. C. It gives a judgment (weak) and a reason (because). A and B report facts.
  4. Describe and argue. First, say what the problem is like, with details. Then take a position on a solution and support it with reasons. The answer needs both parts.
  5. Online classes are easier than in-person classes. Easy is a short adjective, so it becomes easier, without more. The comparing word is than.
