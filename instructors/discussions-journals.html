Setting Expectations for Discussions and Journals

Use this page when you assign discussion posts, journals, or reflections.

About 8 minutes. Includes wording you can copy.

What students experience

Discussions and journals depend on unwritten rules more than any other assignment type. An instructor who has read thousands of posts knows what a good one looks like, so a prompt such as “post your thoughts and respond to two classmates” feels complete. To a student, it leaves most of the assignment undefined.

This affects every student. Some skim the instructions and miss a detail that is there. Others read every word and still cannot find the expectation, because it was never written down. Multilingual learners are often in the second group, and they may also be working from classroom conventions learned in a different educational system.

Expectations about disagreement, self-disclosure, and how to address an instructor differ between educational systems. The CCCC statement asks instructors to check their assignments for unstated cultural assumptions, and it notes that topics drawing on personal experience can be sensitive for some students. The practical response is the same in every case: state the expectation.

Eight practices

  1. Say what the activity is for

    One sentence is enough. “This discussion gives you practice explaining an idea from the reading to other people.”

  2. Say who will read it

    Students write differently for the whole class than for one instructor. For a journal, say plainly whether anyone but you will see it.

  3. Describe the style you expect

    State whether you want complete sentences, whether “I” is acceptable, and whether sources are needed. “Be professional” and “use netiquette” do not answer these questions.

  4. Define a reply

    Give a length and say what a reply must do: answer a classmate’s question, add an example, or explain a different view with a reason. Say that “I agree” alone does not count.

  5. Say that disagreement is welcome, and show how

    A student who was taught not to contradict a classmate or an instructor may need explicit permission. Give them wording: “I see this differently because …”

  6. Say how language is graded

    Decide how much sentence-level accuracy counts in low-stakes writing, and state it either way. A student who does not know may assume that every error costs points, and write less.

  7. Show an annotated model

    One model post with notes shows length, style, and depth faster than a paragraph of description. The transparency framework treats annotated examples as part of the criteria (Winkelmes et al., 2016).

  8. Offer an alternative to personal topics

    When a prompt asks about family, background, or personal difficulty, give a second option that does not, as the CCCC statement recommends.

A practice to try

Ask students to end each first post with an open question for classmates. It gives every student who must “reply to two classmates” a place to start. This is practitioner advice. We did not find a study that tests it directly.

Before and after

What changed

  • “Your thoughts” became one question with two parts.
  • “Thoughtful” became a length, a reason, and an example.
  • “Respond” became a definition of a reply.
  • “Netiquette” became two concrete expectations: a style, and a way to disagree.
  • The grading of language is stated. The line shown is one possible policy. Use your own.

Wording you can copy

Replace the text in brackets.

For journals and reflections

Reader. [Only I will read your journal. / Your journal is shared with your group.]

What to share. You choose what to share. You never have to write about private matters. If a prompt asks about personal experience and you prefer not to answer, you may write about [alternative topic].

Honesty. You may write about what is difficult or not working for you in this course. Honest answers will not lower your grade.

Language. I read your journal for your ideas. [Grammar and spelling are not graded. / Grammar and spelling count for (amount).]

For discussions, in place of “netiquette”

In our discussions:

  • Begin a reply with your classmate’s name.
  • Write in complete sentences. Do not write in all capital letters.
  • You may disagree. Give a reason, and write about the idea, not the person.
  • Jokes and sarcasm are easy to misread in writing. Use them with care.
  • If a post confuses or upsets you, ask the writer a question before you respond, or contact me.
