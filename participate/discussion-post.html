Take part in class

Writing a Discussion Post

Use this page when your class has an online discussion and you need to write your first post.

About 10 minutes. Includes practice with answers.

What a discussion post is

Many college courses have an online discussion board. Your instructor asks a question. Each student writes an answer, called a first post, and the whole class can read it. Then students reply to each other.

This page is about the first post. A discussion is a conversation in writing. It is more careful than a text message and less formal than an essay.

Where a discussion post fits
Text messageDiscussion postEssay
Who reads itA friendYour classmates and your instructorYour instructor, in most classes
SentencesShort pieces and abbreviationsComplete sentencesComplete sentences in organized paragraphs
Using “I”YesYes, in most classesIt depends on the assignment
Your opinionYesYes, with a reasonYes, with reasons and evidence

What instructors expect

Instructors do not always write these expectations down. In most U.S. college classes, they apply to every student.

  • Answer every part of the question. Many discussion questions have two parts, such as “which one, and why?”
  • Give your own opinion, and give a reason for it. Repeating the reading is not enough.
  • Connect your answer to the course. Use an idea from the reading or the lesson.
  • Remember who can read your post. Your whole class can read it. Share only what you are comfortable sharing with classmates.
  • Post early when you can. Classmates need time to read your post and reply.
Key terms
Discussion board:
the place in an online course where students post and reply.
First post:
your own answer to the instructor’s question. It is also called an initial post.
Reply:
a response to a classmate’s post.
Netiquette:
polite and respectful behavior in online communication.

Four parts of a strong first post

  1. Answer. Answer the question in your first sentence. Use some of the words from the question.
  2. Reason. Say why. One clear reason is better than three weak ones.
  3. Support. Add an idea from the reading, an example from your experience, or both.
  4. Question. End with a question for your classmates.
Why end with a question?

A question is an invitation. Classmates who are looking for a post to reply to will find yours easy to answer, and you will get more interesting replies. Do this unless your prompt asks for something different.

Language focus: asking a question

Language focus

Word order in questions

In most English questions, a helping verb such as do, does, did, has, or would comes before the subject.

How a question is built
Question wordHelping verbSubjectMain verb and the rest
Howdoyouplan your study time?
Whatwouldyoudo in this situation?
Whydoesthis strategywork for you?
(none)Hasanyone elsehad this experience?
Watch for this

After do, does, or did, the main verb has no ending. Write Why does this strategy work? Do not write “Why does this strategy works?”

Closed and open questions

A closed question can be answered in one word. An open question needs an explanation. Open questions get better replies.

  • Closed: Do you agree?
  • Open: What is one reason someone might disagree?

Open questions often begin with how, why, or what.

Practice

  1. A student posts: “I think the weekly schedule is the best strategy. Do you agree?” Which two of the four parts are missing?
  2. Which closing question will get the most useful replies?
    • A. Do you agree?
    • B. Thoughts?
    • C. What do you do when two assignments are due on the same day?
  3. Correct this question: “Why this strategy works for you?”
  4. Correct this question: “Anyone has tried this strategy?”
Show suggested answers
  1. The reason and the support. The post gives an answer and a question. It does not say why, and it has no example or idea from the reading.
  2. C. A classmate can answer it from experience, and the answer needs more than one word. A is a closed question. B is not a complete question, so readers do not know what you are asking.
  3. Why does this strategy work for you? The helping verb does comes before the subject. After does, the main verb has no ending: work.
  4. Has anyone tried this strategy? The helping verb has moves to the front of the question.

Next step

Check your post before you submit it

  1. Did I answer every part of the question?
  2. Did I give a reason?
  3. Did I add an example or an idea from the reading?
  4. Did I end with an open question?
  5. Is my post the right length? Check the word count in the prompt.
