Take part in class

Writing a Journal or Reflection

Use this page when your instructor asks you to write a journal entry or reflect on your learning.

About 10 minutes. Includes practice with answers.

What a journal or reflection is

A journal or reflection is writing about your own learning. Your instructor asks you to think about something you did, read, or experienced. Then you explain what it means to you and what you will do next.

A reflection is not a summary. A summary repeats what the reading or the lesson said. A reflection explains what you noticed, learned, or now think about it.

Summary and reflection
What is comparedSummaryReflection
The main questionWhat did the text say?What did I learn, and what will I do with it?
Using “I”Usually noYes. “I” is expected.
Your experienceNot includedThe center of the writing
Example sentenceThe chapter describes three study methods.I tried the second method this week, and it changed how I study.

What instructors expect

Instructors do not always write these expectations down. In most U.S. college classes, they apply to every student.

  • Be specific. Write about one moment, one task, or one idea. “I learned a lot” does not tell the reader what you learned.
  • Be honest. You may write that something was difficult or that you were confused. Instructors want to know this. An honest reflection is a good reflection.
  • Connect to the course. Use a word, an idea, or a skill from the class.
  • Look forward. End with what you will do next time.
  • Write in complete sentences. The style is less formal than an essay, but it is not a text message.
You choose what to share

A reflection is about your learning. It does not need private details about your life, your family, or your health. If a prompt asks about personal experience and you prefer not to answer, look in the prompt for another option, or ask your instructor for one. This is a normal request.

Key terms
Journal:
a set of short writings about your learning, often written each week.
Reflection:
writing that looks back at an experience and explains what you learned from it.
Low-stakes writing:
writing that counts for few or no points. It is a place to practice and think.
Prompt:
the question or instructions for the assignment.

Before you write, check the prompt for two things: who will read it (only your instructor, or your classmates too) and whether grammar is graded. If the prompt does not say, you may ask.

Three questions that build a reflection

Most reflections answer three questions, in this order.

  1. What happened? Describe one specific experience: a task, a reading, a test, or a class activity. Keep this part short.
  2. What did I learn from it? Explain what you noticed, understood, or now think. This is the most important part. Give it the most space.
  3. What will I do next? Name one specific action for the future.
Watch the balance

A common problem is a long first part and a short second part. If most of your sentences describe what happened, cut some, and add more about what you learned.

Language focus: moving between past, present, and future

Language focus

One reflection, three times

A reflection usually moves through three times: what happened (past), what you think now (present), and what you will do (future). Each part has its own verb tense. A time word at the start of a sentence tells the reader that the time is changing.

Verb tense in each part of a reflection
Part and tenseTime wordsExample
What happened: pastlast week, at first, when I startedAt first, I read the chapter only once.
What I learned: presentnow, today, at this pointNow I understand why notes help me.
What I will do: futurenext time, in the next unit, from now onNext time, I will take notes while I read.
Watch for this

Inside one part, keep the same tense. Change tense when the time changes, and use a time word to show the change. Write Last week I studied alone. Now I study with a partner. Do not write “Last week I study alone.”

One more pattern is common in reflections: I learned that + a complete sentence. For example: I learned that I write better in the morning.

Practice

  1. Which sentence is reflection, not summary?
    • A. The article explains how sleep affects memory.
    • B. After I read the article, I started going to bed one hour earlier before tests.
    • C. The author is a professor at a large university.
  2. Make this sentence more specific: “I learned a lot from this project.”
  3. Correct the verb tense: “Last week I study for the quiz, but I do not finish the review.”
  4. A student’s reflection is eight sentences long. Six sentences describe what happened. One sentence says what the student learned. One sentence gives a plan. What should the student change?
  5. A journal prompt asks you to write about a difficult time in your family. You prefer not to. What can you do?
Show suggested answers
  1. B. It describes what the writer did because of the reading. A summarizes the article. C gives a fact about the author.
  2. One possible answer: “I learned that I need to check my sources before I start writing, not after.” The new sentence names one thing the writer learned. Use I learned that + a complete sentence. Other answers are possible.
  3. Last week I studied for the quiz, but I did not finish the review. Last week shows the past, so both verbs are past tense: studied and did not finish.
  4. Shorten the first part, and add more about what they learned. “What I learned” is the most important part, so it should have the most space. Two sentences about what happened are usually enough.
  5. Look for another option in the prompt, or ask your instructor for one. You never have to share private matters in a reflection. Asking for a different topic is normal and polite. For example: “Could I write about a difficult time at work or at school instead?”

Next step

Check your reflection before you submit it

  1. Did I write about one specific experience?
  2. Did I explain what I learned, and give that part the most space?
  3. Did I connect to an idea or skill from the course?
  4. Did I end with a specific plan?
  5. Did I change verb tense only when the time changed?
  6. Did I share only what I am comfortable sharing?
